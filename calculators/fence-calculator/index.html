About this fence calculator

Fence estimating breaks into three independent counts. Pickets = total inches of fence divided by (picket width + gap). Posts = number of 8-foot sections plus one. Rails = two per section for a standard privacy fence (three for tall or high-wind fences). Get those three right and the materials list is done.

The gap setting is where designs diverge. A true privacy fence uses zero gap; shadowbox and spaced-picket styles use 1–3 inches. Note the gap also changes your picket count — a 2-inch gap on 5.5-inch pickets cuts picket needs by more than a quarter, which is real money on a long run.

Posts are the structural heart. Set them 2–3 feet deep (below frost line in cold climates), in concrete, and use pressure-treated or cedar posts rated for ground contact. A fence fails at the posts years before the pickets give out — this is not the place to economize.

Worked example: materials for a 100-ft privacy fence

Take a 100-ft fence run with 5.5-inch-wide pickets, no gap, and posts every 8 feet. Pickets: 100 ft = 1,200 inches ÷ 5.5 = 218.18, rounded up to 219 pickets. Posts: 100 ÷ 8 = 12.5 sections, rounded up to 13 sections, plus 1 = 14 posts. Rails: 13 sections × 2 rails = 26 rails.

That is the lumber order in one pass: 219 pickets, 14 posts, 26 rails. Add concrete (about one 50-lb bag per post — 14 to 16 bags) and fasteners, and you have the full shopping list. The posts are the part people underbuy: a fence is only as strong as its posts, so do not shave this number.

Buying tips

Pressure-treated posts rated for ground contact are non-negotiable — standard treated lumber rots at the soil line in a few years. For gate posts, step up to 6×6 posts set in extra concrete; gates are heavy and they rack ordinary 4×4 posts within a season or two.

Before you buy anything, confirm the property line (a survey is cheaper than moving a fence), check HOA rules on height and style, and call 811 to mark utilities before you dig — hitting a gas or fiber line turns a weekend project into a very expensive day. Local codes often cap front-yard fences at 3–4 ft and side/back at 6–8 ft.

How to use

  1. Measure the total fence length in feet, following the actual line including gates (subtract gate openings).
  2. Enter picket width (5.5 in for nominal 1×6) and your gap (0 for privacy).
  3. Set post spacing — 8 ft is standard for wood privacy fences.
  4. Press Calculate for pickets, posts, rails, and a concrete rule-of-thumb.

Good to know

  • Straight runs only — measure each straight section separately and add the results.
  • Gates need their own hardware (hinges, latch, gate frame kit) and are excluded from picket counts.
  • Concrete figure is a rule of thumb (~1 bag per hole); verify hole volume with the concrete calculator.
  • Check property lines, HOA rules, and local height/setback codes before you dig — and call 811 for utilities.
  • Set posts at least 2 ft deep (one-third of post height for taller fences) and bell out the bottom of the hole — it resists frost heave far better than a straight-sided hole.
  • Budget about one 50-lb bag of concrete per post for a typical 6-ft privacy fence. Dry-packing (tamping dry mix and letting ground moisture set it) works in many soils and skips the mixing.

Frequently asked questions

How many fence pickets do I need for 100 feet?

With 5.5-inch pickets and no gap: 100 × 12 ÷ 5.5 = 219 pickets (rounded up). Add 5–10% for culls and bad boards.

How far apart should fence posts be?

8 feet on center is standard for wood privacy fences. Wider spacing sags; closer spacing wastes posts and concrete.

How many bags of concrete per fence post?

Roughly one 50–60 lb bag per post hole for a typical 10-inch-diameter, 2.5-foot-deep hole. For exact numbers, measure your hole and use the concrete calculator.

How do you calculate a fence?

Pickets = fence inches ÷ (picket width + gap); posts = sections + 1; rails = 2 per section.

Should there be a gap between fence pickets?

Zero gap for privacy. Spaced pickets (1–3 in gaps) suit decorative or airflow designs and use fewer pickets.

How deep should fence posts be?

At least 2 feet, or one-third of the total post height — a 6-ft fence needs 8-ft posts with 2 ft in the ground. In frost country, set posts below the local frost line or heaving will loosen them within a couple of winters.

How much concrete do I need per fence post?

Roughly one 50-lb bag per post for a standard 10-inch-wide, 2-ft-deep hole. For a 14-post fence, buy 14–16 bags — the extras cover the inevitable hole that ends up wider than planned.

Is 6 ft or 8 ft post spacing better?

8 ft is the standard for wood privacy fences and uses fewer posts and less concrete. 6 ft spacing is stronger — better for tall fences, high-wind areas, or heavy gates — it just costs more. Enter your spacing above and the calculator handles either.

How do I account for a gate in the fence run?

Measure each gate opening separately and subtract it from the total fence length before calculating. A gate needs its own heavier posts (6×6 recommended) set in extra concrete, plus hinges and a latch — budget those separately since this calculator covers the straight run.
