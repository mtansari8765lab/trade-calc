About this wall insulation calculator

Wall insulation is a cavity problem, not a depth problem: the R-value you can reach is capped by the stud depth. A 2×4 wall (3½ in cavity) takes R-13 batts, or R-15 high-density; a 2×6 wall (5½ in cavity) takes R-19, or R-21 high-density. No batt, however good, beats the cavity it sits in — that is the whole difference from an attic, where you can always pile insulation deeper. This calculator takes the wall area, subtracts doors and windows, and divides by the batt coverage (a 15 × 93 in batt covers 9.69 sq ft; a 23 × 93 in batt covers 14.85 sq ft) or converts to dense-pack cellulose weight.

Check the target R-value against your climate zone before you buy. The 2021 IECC puts most US wall targets at R-13 to R-20 for wood-frame walls depending on zone, with R-20+ in the coldest zones — and a 2×4 wall cannot reach the cold-zone targets on cavity insulation alone. Walls that need more than R-21 get it from continuous exterior insulation (rigid foam board outside the sheathing), which also breaks the thermal bridge through the studs. Local code governs; the zone target is the number to check, not the batt label.

Worked example: a 12 × 14 ft room with 8-ft walls, 2×4 studs, 15-in batts. Gross wall area is 2 × (12 + 14) × 8 = 416 sq ft. Openings are one 3 × 7 ft door (21 sq ft) plus two 3 × 5 ft windows (30 sq ft) = 51 sq ft, leaving 365 sq ft net. At 9.69 sq ft per batt that is 38 batts, or 42 batts with a 10% waste allowance. As dense-pack cellulose the same walls need 365 lb — 15 bags of 25 lb — before waste. Enter your own room or wall length above.

Batts and cellulose suit different walls. Batts are cheap, DIY-friendly, and exact — but they only perform when they fill the cavity snugly, with no gaps, folds, or compression; a batt squeezed into a shallow cavity loses R-value. Dense-pack cellulose is blown in and fills irregular cavities, old walls, and around wiring far better, at about 1.0 lb per sq ft for a 2×4 cavity and 1.6 lb for 2×6 (at 3.5 lb/cu ft) — but it needs a blower, a dense-pack nozzle, and a wall that can take the pressure. Either way, air sealing matters as much as R-value: seal the gaps around outlets, windows, and the top and bottom plates first, or the insulation is heating the outdoors. Our attic insulation calculator covers the open attic floor, where depth is not capped; our wall stud calculator counts the framing the insulation sits between.

How to use

  1. Choose how to enter the walls: a room (length × width) for all four walls, or a total wall length for one wall or a run.
  2. Enter the wall height, the stud depth (2×4 or 2×6 — this sets the R-value ceiling), and the batt width for your stud spacing.
  3. Enter the openings area (doors and windows in sq ft) so you do not buy insulation for glass — a 3 × 7 ft door is 21 sq ft, a 3 × 5 ft window is 15.
  4. Pick batts or dense-pack cellulose and a waste allowance, then press Calculate.

Good to know

  • Counts use the full net wall area. Studs, plates, and headers occupy roughly 15% of a framed wall, so the batt count carries a small built-in margin — do not stack extra waste on top of it.
  • R-values are for standard fiberglass batts in the cavity. High-density batts, mineral wool, and spray foam have different R-values per inch — this calculator does not size those.
  • Cellulose weight assumes dense-pack at 3.5 lb/cu ft. Loose-fill and wet-spray densities differ, and bag weights vary by brand — check the bag.
  • Does not account for air sealing, vapor control, or thermal bridging through the studs. Those decide real-world performance as much as the R-value does.
  • Climate-zone R-value targets vary by location and code edition — check the 2021 IECC zone target or local code for your area.

Formula & standards

Formula: Net wall area = (2 × (length + width) × height, or wall length × height) − openings area; batts = ceil(net area ÷ batt coverage × (1 + waste)); cellulose lb = net area × 1.0 (2×4) or 1.6 (2×6) lb/sq ft.

Standard: R-13 (R-15 high-density) is the standard batt for a 2×4 cavity, R-19 (R-21 high-density) for 2×6; R-values are laboratory ratings per ASTM C518. Climate-zone targets follow the 2021 IECC.

Waste allowance: Cutting around outlets, corners, and irregular cavities; framing occupies roughly 15% of the wall, which offsets part of the allowance.

Frequently asked questions

How much insulation do I need for a wall?

Wall area minus doors and windows, divided by the coverage of one batt. A 12 × 14 ft room with 8-ft walls is 416 sq ft gross, 365 sq ft after openings, which is 38 batts of 15-in fiberglass (42 with 10% waste) for 2×4 walls. Enter your own dimensions above.

What R-value can I get in a 2x4 wall?

R-13 with standard batts, or R-15 with high-density batts. The 3½-inch cavity caps it — no batt can beat the depth it sits in. For more, add continuous exterior insulation outside the sheathing.

How many batts do I need for a wall?

Divide the net wall area by the batt coverage: a 15 × 93 in batt covers 9.69 sq ft, a 23 × 93 in batt covers 14.85 sq ft. This calculator does the division and adds your waste allowance.

Batts or blown cellulose for walls?

Batts are cheaper and DIY-friendly but need a snug, gap-free fit to perform. Dense-pack cellulose fills irregular cavities and old walls better and needs a blower. For new, regular stud walls, batts are the usual pick; for retrofits and irregular cavities, cellulose wins.

Do I subtract doors and windows from the wall area?

Yes — insulation does not cover glass. Add up the openings (a 3 × 7 ft door is 21 sq ft, a 3 × 5 ft window is 15) and enter the total; this calculator subtracts it before counting batts.

What if my walls need more than R-21?

Add continuous exterior insulation — rigid foam board outside the sheathing — which also breaks the thermal bridge through the studs. Cavity insulation alone cannot exceed the stud depth, so the extra R-value has to come from outside the cavity.
