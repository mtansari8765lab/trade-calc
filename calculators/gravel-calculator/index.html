About this gravel calculator

Driveways, paths, and shed pads all start with the same volume math: length × width × depth. Quarries sell aggregate by the cubic yard or by weight — a cubic yard of crushed stone weighs roughly 1.4 tons (denser rock like trap rock runs heavier, lightweight aggregates lighter). This calculator reports both so you can compare quotes either way.

Depth is the planning decision. A pedestrian path works at 2–3 inches; a residential driveway wants 4–6 inches of compacted aggregate over a prepared subgrade. And "compacted" is doing work in that sentence — loose gravel settles 20–30%, so a 4-inch loose layer becomes about 3 inches of driveway. Order accordingly.

For driveways, the pro move is layers: larger crusher-run stone at the bottom for structure, smaller angular gravel on top for a tight surface. Round pea gravel looks nice in garden paths but migrates under tires — use angular crushed stone where vehicles drive.

Worked example: gravel for a 50×12 driveway

A 50×12 ft driveway at 4 inches deep: 50 × 12 × (4 ÷ 12) = 200 cubic feet, divided by 27 = 7.41 cubic yards. Suppliers sell by the yard but haul by the ton, so convert: 7.41 × 1.4 tons per yard = 10.37, or about 10.4 tons of crushed stone.

That 10.4-ton figure fits in a single dump-truck load (most carry 10–14 tons), which matters for delivery cost — a second partial load can cost nearly as much in trucking as the first. If your number lands at 11 tons, consider whether 10 tons at a slightly thinner depth works, or just accept the two-load delivery fee in your budget.

Buying tips

Order by cubic yards, not tons. Every quarry converts volume to weight with its own density factor, so quoting in yards keeps supplier quotes comparable — the 1.4 tons-per-yard figure used here is typical for crushed stone, but your supplier’s ticket is the final word. Ask for the price per yard delivered, not just per yard, since hauling is often half the invoice.

Expect 10–15% compaction: a 4-inch loose layer settles to about 3.5 inches after driving on it. Order for the compacted depth you actually want. For driveways, build in two layers — 3–4 inches of larger base stone topped with 2 inches of finer crusher run or #57 stone that locks together. A single thin 2-inch layer ruts out in the first rainy season.

How to use

  1. Measure the length and width of the area in feet.
  2. Enter the loose depth in inches — 4 inches is typical for driveways.
  3. Press Calculate for cubic yards and tons.
  4. Add ~10% for compaction; confirm tons-per-yard with your quarry.

Good to know

  • Tons-per-yard varies by rock type (1.3–1.6 common) — confirm with your supplier.
  • Does not include subgrade prep, geotextile fabric, or edging — all worth budgeting.
  • Irregular areas: measure the bounding rectangle and adjust down for curves.
  • Order by cubic yards, not tons — suppliers convert with their own density factors, and quoting volume keeps quotes comparable. The 1.4 tons/yd³ used here is typical for crushed stone.
  • Expect 10–15% compaction: a 4-inch loose layer settles to about 3.5 inches. Order for the compacted depth you want, or add roughly 10% extra.

Formula & standards

Formula: Cubic yards = length × width × (depth ÷ 12) ÷ 27; tons = cubic yards × density (1.4 tons/yd³ default).

Standard: None — densities are standard supplier figures (sand ~1.3, gravel ~1.4, dense crusher run ~1.5 tons/yd³).

Waste allowance: Compaction and uneven subgrade: a 4-in layer compacts to roughly 3–3.5 in.

Frequently asked questions

How much gravel do I need for a driveway?

A 50×12 ft driveway at 4 inches deep: 50 × 12 × (4/12) = 200 cu ft ≈ 7.4 cubic yards (≈10.4 tons). Add 10% for compaction.

How many tons of gravel are in a cubic yard?

Roughly 1.4 tons per cubic yard for crushed stone. Dense rock runs heavier; confirm with your quarry.

How deep should a gravel driveway be?

4–6 inches of compacted aggregate over a firm subgrade. Less than 4 inches ruts quickly under vehicles.

What is the difference between crushed stone and pea gravel?

Crushed stone is angular and locks together — best for driveways. Pea gravel is rounded and shifts under tires — best for paths and landscaping.

How do you calculate gravel?

Length (ft) × width (ft) × depth (in ÷ 12) = cubic feet; divide by 27 for cubic yards; multiply yards by ~1.4 for tons.

How many tons of gravel are in a cubic yard?

About 1.4 tons per cubic yard for crushed stone or gravel — so 7.41 yards works out to roughly 10.4 tons. Sand runs lighter (about 1.3) and dense crusher run heavier (about 1.5); your supplier’s scale ticket is the final word.

How deep should a gravel driveway be?

4–6 inches total, ideally in two layers: 3–4 inches of larger base stone topped with 2 inches of finer crusher run or #57 stone that locks together under tires. A thin 2-inch layer looks fine on day one and ruts out in the first rainy season.

How is gravel delivered?

By dump truck, typically 10–14 tons per load — a 10-ton driveway order arrives in one truck. Small jobs can buy 0.5-cu-ft bags, but at 54 bags per cubic yard, bulk delivery is far cheaper once you need more than a yard or two.

Does the type of gravel change the calculation?

The volume math is identical — only weight and price change. Pea gravel, #57 stone, and crusher run all cover the same area at the same depth. Pick based on drainage and how the stone packs, not the calculator: crusher run locks together for driveways, pea gravel shifts and suits walkways and beds.
