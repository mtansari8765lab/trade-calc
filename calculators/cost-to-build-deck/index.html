About this deck cost estimator

Deck quotes cluster around a per-square-foot number that bakes in framing, decking, and labor — then railings, stairs, and site conditions push the real total around. This estimator applies your rate tier to your deck's area and brackets the result ±20–25%, which is about the spread between a simple rectangle and one with stairs, angles, and a railing package.

The rate tiers tell the real story. DIY pressure-treated (~$20/sq ft in materials) is the budget path if you have the skills and weekends. Pro-built pressure-treated (~$30/sq ft) is the mainstream choice. Composite (~$55/sq ft installed) costs more up front but skips the annual staining ritual. Premium designs with curves, multiple levels, or integrated lighting land higher still.

Two budget blind spots: railings (often $30–$60+ per linear foot installed — a 16×12 deck can need 40+ feet of it) and permits/footings (frost-depth footings and inspections add real money in cold climates). Neither is in the per-square-foot math — add them mentally to the top of the range.

Worked example: pricing a 16×12 deck

A 16×12 ft deck is 192 square feet. At a mid-range rate of $30 per square foot for pressure-treated pine, the planning estimate is 192 × $30 = $5,760. This calculator shows a range around that midpoint: roughly $4,608 on the low end (simple, ground-level, budget materials) to $7,200 on the high end (taller deck, railings, stairs, or pricier materials).

Swap the decking to composite and the picture changes fast: composite typically runs 2–3× the installed cost of pressure-treated pine, so that same 192 sq ft deck often lands between $9,000 and $14,000 professionally built. That is why the single biggest lever in this estimate is the rate you enter — get it from local quotes, not national averages, and the range tightens up considerably.

Getting a useful number

Start with the default rate to sanity-check a contractor bid: if a bid comes in far outside the calculator’s range, ask what is driving it — sometimes it is legitimate (difficult access, deep frost footings), sometimes it is padding. Always collect three local quotes; deck labor markets vary enormously by region, and the spread between the highest and lowest bid for identical work is routinely 30–50%.

Do not forget the costs outside the boards: permits typically run $100–$500, and most jurisdictions require one for any deck attached to the house or more than 30 inches off the ground. Stairs, railings, and lighting are often quoted separately — a full railing package alone can add $1,500–$4,000 to a mid-size deck.

How to use

  1. Enter your deck length and width in feet.
  2. Pick the cost tier matching your build (DIY vs. pro, wood vs. composite).
  3. Press Calculate for the cost range.
  4. Add railings, stairs, and permits on top — then get 3 local quotes.

Good to know

  • Excludes railings, stairs, permits, demolition of an old deck, and lighting.
  • Per-square-foot rates vary by region and site access — treat as a planning bracket.
  • Structural design (beam spans, footing depth, ledger attachment) must follow local code — this is a cost tool, not an engineering tool.
  • Material choice is the biggest cost lever: pressure-treated pine is the budget option, cedar/redwood sits in the middle, and composite/PVC typically costs 2–3× as much installed as treated pine.
  • Stairs, railings, and deck height add cost fast — an elevated deck needing tall posts, beams, and full railings can run 40–60% more per square foot than a low platform deck.

Formula & standards

Formula: Estimated cost = deck area × cost per sq ft, shown as a range (−20% to +25% around the entered rate).

Standard: None — a planning estimate from typical 2026 U.S. pricing, not a standard.

Waste allowance: No waste field on this estimator; railings, stairs, permits, and footings move real quotes.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to build a deck?

Pressure-treated decks professionally built typically run $25–$40 per sq ft; composite $50–$70+. A 16×12 deck (192 sq ft) often lands at $5,000–$8,000 in wood or $10,000–$14,000+ in composite, before railings and stairs.

Is it cheaper to build a deck yourself?

Materials-only DIY typically costs 40–60% of a pro quote — but budget your weekends honestly, plus tool rentals, permits, and the learning curve on structural details.

How much does a composite deck cost vs wood?

Composite decking materials cost roughly 2–3× pressure-treated wood, but need no staining and last longer. Break-even on maintenance is often 5–10 years.

Do I need a permit to build a deck?

Usually yes for attached or elevated decks; rules vary by municipality. Ground-level floating decks under a certain height are sometimes exempt — check with your local building department before buying lumber.

How do you estimate deck cost?

Deck area × per-square-foot rate for your build tier, then add railings (~$30–60+/linear ft), stairs, and permits on top.

How much does a 12x16 deck cost?

A 12×16 ft deck is 192 square feet. At $30 per square foot (mid-range pressure-treated), expect about $5,760, with a planning range of roughly $4,608–$7,200. The same deck in composite often lands between $9,000 and $14,000 installed. Enter your dimensions above for your exact numbers.

What affects deck cost per square foot the most?

Decking material first, then deck height (elevated decks need more structure), railings and stairs, footing depth for your frost line, and local labor rates. Permits typically add $100–$500 on top.

Does building a deck require a permit?

In most US jurisdictions, yes — especially for decks attached to the house or more than 30 inches off the ground. Permit fees are usually $100–$500, and the inspector checks footings, ledger attachment, and railings. Unpermitted decks can stall a future home sale.

Will a new deck raise my home’s value?

A well-built deck typically recoups roughly half to two-thirds of its cost at resale, according to remodeling industry surveys — but treat it as a lifestyle upgrade first and an investment second. This estimator is a planning tool, not a quote: always get local contractor bids before budgeting.
