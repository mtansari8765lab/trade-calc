About this house construction cost estimator

A house budget is four multiplications plus a buffer. Structure (foundation, framing, walls) and finishes (flooring, kitchen, baths, paint) and services (plumbing, electrical, HVAC) are priced per square foot of total floor area; roofing is priced per square foot of footprint, because the roof covers the footprint once no matter how many floors sit under it. Add a lump sum for site and other costs, then a contingency for the surprises every build meets. That is all this estimator does — the rates are yours, so the result is only as good as the rates you bring. Our deck cost estimator and concrete slab cost estimator work the same way for single projects.

Your rates are the whole estimate, so get them from your market, not from a website. Ask two or three local builders or material dealers for current per-square-foot rates, and ask for grey structure and finished rates separately — the gap between a bare structure and a finished house is usually larger than the structure itself. Rates move with the city, the material quality, and the year: a rate from last year or another city is a stale rate. If a field is left blank, the calculator uses a clearly labelled example placeholder rate ($85 structure, $45 finishes, $12 roofing, $25 services per sq ft) and says so in the result — those numbers are arithmetic examples, not market data, and a real budget must replace them. As a published benchmark only, the NAHB 2024 Cost of Constructing a Home survey put the US national average at $162 per sq ft for construction (excluding land) — useful as a sanity check on your own rates, not as a substitute for them.

Worked example: a 40 × 30 ft house, 2 floors = 1,200 sq ft footprint and 2,400 sq ft of floor area. At the example rates, structure is 2,400 × $85 = $204,000; finishes 2,400 × $45 = $108,000; roofing 1,200 × $12 = $14,400; services 2,400 × $25 = $60,000; plus $5,000 site costs = $391,400 subtotal. A 10% contingency adds $39,140, for an estimated total of $430,540, or $179.39 per sq ft. Enter your own dimensions and rates above for your house.

What this estimate does not include: the land, design and engineering fees, permits (unless you put them in the site lump sum), financing and carrying costs, driveways and landscaping beyond what you enter, and furniture. Those commonly add 25–40% on top of construction for an all-in budget, so do not compare this number to an all-in price. And keep the contingency: 10–15% is standard practice, because soil conditions, material delays, and change orders move real builds off their first budget.

How to use

  1. Enter your building length and width in feet and the number of floors.
  2. Enter your own per-square-foot rates for structure, finishes, roofing, and services — from local builders or dealers. Blank rates use labelled example placeholders, not market prices.
  3. Add a lump sum for site and other costs (permits, driveway, fees) and pick a contingency (10% is standard).
  4. Press Calculate for the itemized estimate, total, and cost per square foot — then get itemized local quotes to compare.

Good to know

  • Uses YOUR rates only. Blank rates fall back to labelled example placeholders ($85/$45/$12/$25 per sq ft) that are not market data — replace them before budgeting.
  • Roofing is priced per square foot of footprint at a flat rate; steep or complex roofs cost more per footprint square foot.
  • Excludes land, design/engineering, financing, and landscaping unless you add them to the site lump sum.
  • Planning estimate, not a contractor quote. Two builders can price the same house 20% apart and both be honest — the difference is scope, materials, and overhead.

Formula & standards

Formula: Line item = area × your rate per sq ft (roofing uses footprint area); total = structure + finishes + roofing + services + site costs, plus contingency.

Standard: None — a planning estimate from rates you enter; no code or standard prices a house.

Waste allowance: Contingency (10–15% is standard practice) covers price changes and unknowns; it is not a waste allowance, and the result is not a contractor quote.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to build a house per square foot?

There is no single number: it depends on location, finish level, and what is included. As a published benchmark only, the NAHB 2024 survey put the US national average at $162 per sq ft for construction (excluding land). Get your own local rates from two or three builders and enter them above — your rates, not a national average, are what your budget should rest on.

What should an itemized house estimate include?

At minimum: structure (foundation, framing, walls), finishes (flooring, kitchen, baths, paint), roofing, services (plumbing, electrical, HVAC), site and other costs (permits, driveway, fees), and a contingency. This estimator shows each line separately so you can see which one dominates your total.

How much contingency should I add to a house build?

10–15% of construction cost is standard practice. Use 10% for a straightforward build on a known lot, 15% for a complex site, an unusual design, or a first build where surprises are likelier.

Does this estimate include land, permits, and design fees?

No. Land, design and engineering, financing, and landscaping are excluded unless you add them to the site lump sum. Those commonly add 25–40% on top of construction for an all-in budget, so do not compare this number to an all-in price.

Why are the default rates called example placeholders?

Because construction rates vary by city, material quality, and year, and no website can know your local rate. Blank rate fields use $85 structure, $45 finishes, $12 roofing, and $25 services per sq ft purely so the arithmetic can run — the result names them as placeholders, and a real budget must replace them with local quotes.

How do I find my local per-square-foot rates?

Ask two or three local builders for itemized per-square-foot rates, and ask material dealers for current prices on the big lines (framing lumber, roofing, fixtures). Ask for grey-structure and finished rates separately — the gap between them is usually larger than the structure cost itself.

Is this estimate a contractor quote?

No — it is a planning estimate from rates you enter. A real quote is itemized by a builder who has seen your plans, your lot, and your material choices. Use this number to sanity-check quotes, not to set your final budget.
