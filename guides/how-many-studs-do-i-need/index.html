How Many Studs Do I Need? Spacing, Plates & a Worked Example

Planning estimate, not a framing plan. Stud count is one division plus the lumber people forget: plates and opening studs. This guide gives you the formula, the spacing rules, and a worked 12-foot wall — then run your own wall through our wall stud calculator.

The stud formula

For a straight wall run, the field-stud count is: studs = wall length in inches ÷ spacing, rounded up, + 1 for the far end stud. The +1 is the part people forget — the division alone only counts interior spaces plus one end.

Common lengths at 16-inch spacing: 8 ft → 7 studs, 10 ft → 9, 12 ft → 10, 16 ft → 13, 20 ft → 16. At 24-inch spacing the same walls need 5, 6, 7, 9, and 11. Add 10% for cuts and waste to get the buy list.

16-inch vs 24-inch spacing

16 inches on center is the standard for load-bearing walls: greater rigidity, better drywall support (drywall edges land on studs every 4 feet either way, but 16-inch spacing halves the unsupported span), and required by many jurisdictions for exterior framing. 24 inches on center uses roughly 25% less lumber and is acceptable per IRC for many interior non-load-bearing partitions and some exterior configurations — but check local code before using it on a bearing wall. 12-inch spacing is for heavy loads and tile walls.

Plates: the lumber you forget

Every wall needs three times its length in plate lumber: a double top plate plus a sole (bottom) plate, all in the same stud lumber (usually 2×4). The double top plate distributes loads and lets plate joints overlap for continuity. A 12-ft wall needs 36 linear feet of plates — five 8-ft studs' worth — before you count a single vertical stud.

Openings: kings, jacks, and headers

Each door or window opening adds about 4 studs: 2 king studs (full height, outside the opening) and 2 jack studs (trimmer studs under the header). Headers themselves are separate lumber, sized by span and load — as a rough guide for roof/ceiling-only exterior bearing walls: up to 3 ft span takes (2) 2×4, 3–5 ft takes (2) 2×6, 5–7 ft takes (2) 2×8, and 7–8 ft takes (2) 2×10. Confirm header spans with local code; load-bearing and multi-story walls need larger headers.

Worked example: 12-foot wall with one door

  1. Field studs. 144 in ÷ 16 = 9 spaces + 1 end stud = 10 studs.
  2. Plates. 12 ft × 3 = 36 linear feet.
  3. Opening. One door adds 4 studs (2 king + 2 jack) — total 14.
  4. Buy list. 14 × 1.10 = 15.4 → 16 studs, plus the header lumber for the door.

Corners and intersections

Straight-wall math does not count corner and intersection framing: three-stud corners and ladder blocking give drywall something to fasten to where walls meet. Budget a few extra studs per corner and per T-intersection — your drywaller will thank you, and the calculator's waste allowance only partly covers them.

Frequently asked questions

How many studs do I need for a 10-foot wall?

At 16-inch spacing: 9 field studs (120 ÷ 16 = 7.5, rounded up to 8 spaces, plus 1 end stud) — buy 10 with 10% waste. Plates add 30 linear feet.

What is standard stud spacing?

16 inches on center is standard for load-bearing walls. 24 inches suits many non-load-bearing partitions; 12 inches is for heavy loads and tile walls.

How many studs per door or window opening?

Plan on 4 extra studs per opening: 2 king studs and 2 jack studs. Headers and cripples are additional lumber.

How much plate lumber does a wall need?

Three times the wall length in linear feet: a double top plate plus a sole plate, in the same stud lumber.

Should I use 2x4 or 2x6 studs?

2x4 is standard for interior partitions. Use 2x6 for walls carrying plumbing drain stacks, walls needing thicker insulation or sound batts, and tall walls over 10 feet.

Price the rest of the wall

Once the framing is counted, figure the skin: how many drywall sheets you need, and board feet and cost for the lumber.
