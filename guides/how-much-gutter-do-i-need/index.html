How Much Gutter Do I Need? Length, Downspouts & Worked Examples

Planning estimate, not a gutter quote. Gutter quantity starts from one measurement — the roof edges that actually get gutters — and the downspout rule that prevents overflow. This guide gives you both — then run your house through our gutter calculator.

Measuring roof edges

Walk the house and measure every horizontal roof edge where a gutter will collect water — usually the eaves on the front and back plus any low sides — and add the runs together. Gable ends without eaves do not get gutters. For a rectangular house, the guttered runs are typically one long side plus the two short sides, with the matching long side on the back adding the same again if it has a drip edge. A 40 × 25 ft house has a 130 ft roofline: 2 × (40 + 25). Typical houses run 150–250 linear feet total: a 1,000 sq ft house about 100–120 ft, a 1,500 sq ft house about 120–150 ft, a 2,000 sq ft house about 150–180 ft, and a 2,500 sq ft house about 180–220 ft.

Downspout spacing

The standard rule: one downspout every 30–40 feet of gutter, never more than 50 feet per run, plus one near each outside corner and one at each side of a valley. On a 40 ft front run, one downspout at the corner plus one in the middle keeps the gutter from overflowing in a downpour. More downspouts cost a little more but reduce overflow risk — and a long run with a single downspout at one end is the most common gutter sizing mistake. Hangers run one every 2 feet.

5-inch vs 6-inch

5-inch gutters are standard for most homes. 6-inch gutters suit steep roofs and heavy-rain regions, and handle more water per foot. Profile matters too: K-style gutters (flat back, decorative front) hold more water per inch of width than half-round gutters of the same nominal size, because the flat bottom and near-vertical sides carry more volume; half-round gutters appear most often on older homes and bungalows, where debris slides out more easily but capacity is lower. Most new US construction uses K-style aluminum, so confirm which profile your home has before ordering.

Sections and waste

Sectional gutter comes in 10-foot lengths: divide total length by 10 and round up, because sections trim shorter but never longer. Add roughly 10% extra material for corners, seams, and waste. Seamless gutters, cut on site to eliminate most seams, cost more than sectional but leak less: sectional runs about $2–$5 per foot in materials, seamless installed about $4–$10 per foot (aluminum $3–$8 material, $7–$20 installed; vinyl $2–$5 material; copper $20–$40 material).

Worked examples

  1. 40 × 25 ft house: 130 ft roofline → 13 sections, 4 downspouts at 35 ft spacing, 65 hangers.
  2. 2,000 sq ft house, ~160 ft gutter: 16 sections, 5 downspouts at 35 ft spacing (160 ÷ 35 = 4.57 → 5), 80 hangers.
  3. 1,500 sq ft house, ~140 ft gutter: 14 sections, 4 downspouts, 70 hangers.

Frequently asked questions

How many linear feet of gutter does a house need?

Most homes need 150–250 linear feet. A 40 × 25 ft house has a 130 ft roofline — 13 ten-foot sections. A 2,000 sq ft house typically needs 150–180 feet.

How far apart should downspouts be?

Every 30–40 feet of gutter, never more than 50 feet per run, plus one near each outside corner and one at each side of a valley.

How many downspouts do I need?

Divide total gutter length by your spacing and round up, with a minimum of 2: a 130 ft run at 35 ft spacing needs 4 downspouts.

5-inch or 6-inch gutters?

5-inch is standard for most homes. 6-inch suits steep roofs and heavy-rain regions, and handles more water per foot.

How much does gutter installation cost?

Seamless aluminum runs about $4–$10 per linear foot installed; DIY sectional gutter runs about $2–$5 per foot in materials.

The rest of the roofing job

Gutters finish the roof edge — figure shingle bundles for the roof itself and the cost to shingle the roof when budgeting the whole job.
