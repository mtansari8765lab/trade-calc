How Much Wallpaper Do I Need? Roll Math & Measuring Guide

Planning estimate, not a quote. Under-order wallpaper and you're stuck waiting on a second dye lot that doesn't quite match; over-order and you've paid for rolls you'll never hang. This guide shows how to measure correctly, how rolls are actually sized, why pattern repeat changes the math, and works a full room example. Painting instead (or as well)? Our paint calculator covers the gallons side.

How wallpaper is sold: single vs double rolls

Wallpaper comes in single rolls and double rolls — and the naming confuses everyone once:

  • US single roll: 20.5 inches wide × 33 ft long = ~56 sq ft gross. After trimming top and bottom of each strip, figure ~50 sq ft of usable coverage.
  • US double roll: same width, roughly double the length (~66 ft) — one continuous roll, not two rolls. Usable coverage ~100 sq ft.
  • European roll: typically 21 in × 33 ft (~57 sq ft gross), sold as singles in most cases.

The "usable" numbers are the ones that matter: gross square footage ignores the trimming every strip needs. Always estimate from usable coverage, not the gross figure on the label.

Step 1: measure the walls (not the floor)

The classic mistake is measuring the room's floor area. Wallpaper covers walls, so measure each wall separately:

  1. Measure the width of each wall in feet, and the ceiling height.
  2. Multiply width × height per wall, and add the walls together.
  3. Subtract full door and window areas (a standard door is 3×7 ft = 21 sq ft; measure your windows).
  4. Don't subtract small things like outlets and switches — the paper you "save" there is eaten by trimming waste.

Measure twice, especially around fireplaces, built-ins, and sloped ceilings — irregular walls are where estimates go wrong. For a sloped ceiling, measure the wall at its tallest point and treat the slope conservatively.

Step 2: understand pattern repeat

Pattern repeat is the vertical distance before the design repeats — printed on the label, e.g. "repeat: 21 in." Here's why it costs you paper: each strip must be cut so its pattern aligns with the neighboring strip. With a 21-inch repeat on an 8-ft wall, you can't just cut 8-ft strips back to back; each strip effectively consumes ceiling height plus up to one full repeat of alignment waste.

  • No pattern / random match: minimal waste — strips cut to wall height plus a few inches of trim.
  • Straight match, small repeat (under ~12 in): add roughly 10% to your roll count.
  • Large repeat (18–24+ in) or half-drop match: add 20–25%, or simply one extra roll — half-drop patterns (where alternate strips are offset by half a repeat) are the thirstiest of all.

If the label doesn't list a repeat, assume the worst for a patterned paper and ask the retailer before ordering.

Worked example: 12×10 ft room, 8-ft ceilings

  1. Wall area. Two 12-ft walls: 2 × (12 × 8) = 192 sq ft. Two 10-ft walls: 2 × (10 × 8) = 160 sq ft. Total: 352 sq ft.
  2. Subtract openings. One door (3×7 = 21) + one window (4×5 = 20) = 41 sq ft. Net: 352 − 41 = 311 sq ft.
  3. Single rolls. 311 ÷ 50 usable sq ft = 6.22 → round up to 7 single rolls.
  4. Double rolls. 311 ÷ 100 = 3.11 → round up to 4 double rolls.
  5. Pattern adjustment. Large repeat or half-drop? Add one more roll: 8 singles or 5 doubles.

General formula: rolls = (total wall sq ft − openings) ÷ usable sq ft per roll, always rounded up, plus one for big repeats. Wallpaper math only ever rounds in one direction.

The dye lot rule

Buy all your rolls at once, from the same dye lot — the batch number printed on the label. Color shifts slightly between production runs, and two lots hung side by side can show a visible seam of mismatched color that no amount of careful hanging fixes. Check the lot numbers before you leave the store (or before the delivery driver leaves). The extra roll from the worked example doubles as insurance: if a strip gets damaged during hanging, a same-lot spare saves the project.

Rough cost expectations

Wallpaper pricing spans enormously: basic vinyl from home centers runs roughly $25–$60 per double roll, mid-range designer papers $80–$200, and high-end or hand-printed papers climb past that (2026 US retail — confirm locally). Our 4-double-roll example is therefore anywhere from ~$100 to ~$800 in paper alone, before paste, tools, and primer. This is a planning range, not a quote — and if you're hiring the hanging out, labor often exceeds the paper cost, so get local quotes.

When to hire a paperhanger

DIY hanging is very doable on straight walls with a random-match paper. Consider a pro for: half-drop or large-repeat patterns (alignment is unforgiving), stairwells and tall walls, expensive paper where one ruined strip costs real money, and plaster walls that need lining paper first. A bad hang on good paper looks worse than a good hang on cheap paper.

Frequently asked questions

How much wallpaper do I need for a 12x12 room?

A 12×12 ft room with 8-ft ceilings has about 384 sq ft of wall before openings. After a door and window (~41 sq ft), you need roughly 7 single rolls or 4 double rolls — plus one extra roll for a large pattern repeat.

How many square feet does a roll of wallpaper cover?

A US single roll (20.5 in × 33 ft) is 56 sq ft gross but covers about 50 sq ft usable after trimming. A double roll covers about 100 sq ft usable. Pattern repeat reduces usable coverage further.

What is pattern repeat in wallpaper?

The vertical distance before the design repeats. Each strip must align with its neighbor, so larger repeats waste more paper — a 24-inch repeat can waste a foot or more per strip, and half-drop matches waste the most.

Should I subtract doors and windows when measuring wallpaper?

Yes — measure each wall, then subtract full door and window areas. But keep the standard rounding-up: offcuts around openings rarely reuse cleanly, so don't subtract and then skip the waste allowance too.

What is a double roll of wallpaper?

One continuous roll roughly double the length of a single roll (~66 ft vs 33 ft) at the same width — not two rolls. It means fewer rolls to buy and everything comes from a single dye lot.

Why do I need wallpaper from the same dye lot?

Colors shift slightly between production batches. Rolls from different lots hung side by side can show a visible mismatched seam. Buy all rolls at once and verify the lot numbers match.
